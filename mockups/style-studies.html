Three ways the workshop could look

The same level runs in all three styles. Two pattern cards feed a Mix vat, then an Invert vat, and each result is woven into the tapestry one stitch per tick. It is the Third Color recipe: give it two different primaries and it returns the missing one.

The reserved eight

Every drop carries three dots, one per primary: top for red, right for yellow, left for blue. A filled dot means that primary is in the paint. Turn on the colorblind check to see the whole page in grey and judge whether the dots still carry the meaning.

Critter Workshop My pick

Bloons TD 6 meets a storybook dye works. Chunky outlines, wooden tubs with faces, drops that bulge through glass tubes.

Strengths
Instant appeal for kids. Each component gets a personality you remember it by. Everything here is drawn in code, with no image files.
Risks
Every new component needs a character and its animations. On big machines the faces must stay out of the way.

Pixel Workshop

A cozy retro handheld. The whole world sits on the same coarse pixel grid as the tapestry.

Strengths
Cheap, consistent sprites. Crisp at any size. Strong nostalgia for adult players.
Risks
Reads as retro indie rather than a kids' game. The dots get tiny. The woven picture stops feeling special when everything is pixels.

Paper Minimal

Mini Motorways or a transit map. Thin ink lines, round stations, and color only where it means something.

Strengths
Clearest on large machines. The signals and the tapestry get all the attention. The easiest style to build and scale.
Risks
Little warmth for kids. Nothing to fall in love with besides the puzzle itself.

Mockup only: no sound, and the machine is fixed. Woven tapestry cells skip the dots in all three styles. Whether the finished picture should show them too is still an open question.
